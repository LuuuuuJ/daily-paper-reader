# Learning Bidirectional Causal Interactions with Heteroscedastic Neural Networks 

# Masahiro Tanaka ∗

# July 27, 2026 

Abstract 

Estimating contemporaneous bidirectional interactions from observational data is dif-ficult because each outcome is endogenous to the other, while flexible regressions may capture only reduced-form dependence. This paper proposes SEM-DNN, a heteroscedastic neural simultaneous-equation estimator that learns reciprocal structural interactions with-out external instruments. Identification exploits conditional covariance diagonalization: when structural shocks have zero conditional means, are conditionally uncorrelated given predetermined covariates, and exhibit nonproportional conditional variances, only the true interaction coefficients diagonalize the conditional residual covariance across the feature space. The method jointly approximates nonlinear structural mean functions and feature-dependent variances using a diagonal Gaussian quasi-likelihood that incorporates the simultaneous-system Jacobian. We establish unique identification and positive-definite local curvature of the profiled population criterion and show that, under neural-profile compatibility con-ditions, the implemented neural criterion inherits this curvature despite nonunique net-work parameterizations. The coefficients admit a causal interpretation when the structural equations represent autonomous mechanisms that remain invariant under the relevant inter-ventions. Monte Carlo experiments with nonlinear, high-dimensional nuisance functions and non-Gaussian shocks show that SEM-DNN recovers structural effects more reliably than parametric, kernel-based, and separate-equation neural alternatives as information in-creases, although at greater computational cost. An application to ready-to-eat cereal scan-ner data illustrates how the method can study contemporaneous price–sales feedback and assess identification strength, residual diagonalization, variance calibration, and optimiza-tion sensitivity. The approach may therefore be useful for bidirectional feedback systems in which credible instruments are unavailable but informative heteroscedastic variation is present. Keywords: causal learning, simultaneous equations, heteroscedasticity, neural networks

Many empirical and artificial systems involve contemporaneous bidirectional interactions. For example, online platforms induce feedback between users and advertisers, and policy variables

In such settings, the object of interest is not merely the prediction of one outcome from another, but rather the recovery of the directed structural interactions governing their joint determination. When the structural equations are interpreted as autonomous and interventionally invariant mechanisms, these in-teractions have a causal interpretation. This paper studies the problem of learning bidirectional causal effects between two scalar outcomes from observational data. We consider the simultaneous structural system 

y1i = γ1y2i + f1(xi) + ε1i, (1) 

y2i = γ2y1i + f2(xi) + ε2i, (2) where f1 and f2 are unknown nonlinear functions of observed features. The parameters γ1 and 

γ2 are the direct structural interaction coefficients of primary interest. Under the maintained structural causal interpretation, γ1 measures the direct effect from y2 to y1, and γ2 measures the direct effect from y1 to y2. The goal is to estimate γ1 and γ2 while allowing flexible, high-dimensional, and nonlinear nuisance functions. The main difficulty is simultaneity. A naive regression of y1 on y2 and x treats y2 as an exogenous covariate, even though y2 is itself determined partly by y1. The same problem arises in the reverse equation. Consequently, flexible single-equation neural regressions may learn reduced-form predictive dependence without recovering the structural interaction parameters. We propose a neural simultaneous-equation estimator that uses conditional heteroscedas-ticity as the source of identification. The key idea is that the correct structural coefficients transform the observed outcomes into residuals whose conditional covariance matrix is diago-nal. At an incorrect value of (γ1, γ 2), the transformed residuals generally mix the two structural shocks, producing conditional residual correlation. If the two structural shock variances vary nonproportionally over the feature space, an incorrect transformation cannot diagonalize the conditional covariance matrix for all covariate values. Thus, conditional second moments pro-vide information about the two directions of contemporaneous causality. To operationalize this idea, we model both the structural mean functions and the conditional variance functions with neural networks. The conditional variances are specified as 

Var( εki | xi) = gk(xi), k = 1 , 2,

with 

gk(xi) = gmin + ψ{egk(xi)}, k = 1 , 2.

Here, ψ(·) is the softplus link. The variance networks are not used merely as uncertainty estima-tors; rather, they allow the model to learn the heteroscedastic variation used for identification. The model is trained by maximizing a diagonal Gaussian quasi-likelihood for the simultaneous system, including the Jacobian term associated with the transformation between observed out-comes and structural shocks. The resulting estimator, which we call the simultaneous-equation deep neural network estimator (SEM-DNN), jointly learns nonlinear mean functions, feature-dependent variances, and bidirectional structural effects. This paper makes three contributions. First, it develops a neural simultaneous-equation framework for estimating bidirectional structural interactions in observational systems with nonlinear nuisance components. Under explicit structural invariance assumptions, these in-teractions can be interpreted as direct causal effects. Second, it adapts heteroscedasticity-based identification to a flexible neural setting, showing that nonproportional variation in the two con-ditional variances can identify the two structural directions after the nuisance functions are pro-filed out. Third, it provides a Monte Carlo evaluation comparing SEM-DNN with parametric, 2kernel-based, and naive single-equation neural alternatives. The simulation results show that SEM-DNN achieves lower bias and root mean squared error (RMSE) than the competing ap-proaches in nonlinear designs, with especially clear gains when the structural mean and variance functions are difficult to approximate parametrically. This paper contributes to the literature on causal machine learning for systems with mutu-ally dependent outcomes [4, 8]. Much of causal machine learning combines flexible predic-tion methods with structural assumptions that define causal or counterfactual targets. Neural instrumental-variable methods [5], such as Deep IV, use deep networks to learn causal response functions in the presence of endogeneity, but their identification strategy relies on exclusion restrictions: an instrument must shift the endogenous variable while being excluded from the structural outcome equation. Our approach addresses a different identification environment. Instead of using instruments, SEM-DNN exploits conditional second moments. The identify-ing information comes from feature-dependent changes in the structural shock variances, while neural networks are used to approximate the unknown conditional mean and variance functions. The proposed approach is also related to causal discovery in structural equation models. Classical methods such as LiNGAM identify linear acyclic structure by exploiting non-Gaussian disturbances [14], while extensions to cyclic systems use non-Gaussianity [9] or observational and interventional equilibrium data [2] to learn feedback relations. More generally, cyclic struc-tural causal models require explicit solvability conditions to ensure well-defined observational and interventional distributions. SEM-DNN is not a general causal-discovery procedure over an unrestricted graph class. It assumes a reciprocal two-node structural system and estimates its two interaction coefficients from conditional second-moment variation. Related location-scale causal-discovery methods also exploit heteroscedasticity, but assume a unidirectional cause– effect model [7]. Finally, the neural networks in SEM-DNN provide flexible nuisance-function approximation rather than causal identification by themselves, consistent with the general dis-tinction between neural expressiveness and causal learnability [18]. The paper is also related to the econometric literature on simultaneous equations and identifi-cation through heteroscedasticity. In this literature, Rigobon [12] introduced heteroscedasticity-based identification using regime-dependent variance shifts, and Milunovich and Yang [10] proposed a quasi-maximum likelihood estimator for heteroscedastic simultaneous-equation sys-tems. Tanaka [16] extended this line of work to online estimation and large-scale kernel learning. SEM-DNN augments this heteroscedastic simultaneous-equation approach with deep neural networks. This extension connects the method to recent work on heteroscedastic neural regres-sion [3, 13, 15, 17], which examines the instability and calibration problems that can arise when mean and variance networks are trained jointly. To illustrate the empirical use of the proposed framework, we apply SEM-DNN to the ready-to-eat cereal category in the Dominick’s scanner database. The application examines contem-poraneous feedback between product movement and package price using historical sales and pricing information, product attributes, and store characteristics as predetermined covariates. Stores are separated into training, validation, and diagnostic partitions so that model selection is conducted independently of the final diagnostic assessment. The fitted conditional variances exhibit nonproportional variation, providing heteroscedastic information relevant to separating the two interaction directions. At the same time, dispersion across neural-network training seeds and the held-out residual-diagonalization and variance-calibration diagnostics reveal material empirical uncertainty. The application is therefore intended to demonstrate the complete esti-mation and diagnostic workflow of SEM-DNN, rather than to provide definitive causal elasticity estimates for cereal demand and pricing. More broadly, SEM-DNN may be useful in observational systems in which two outcomes 3adjust to one another within the same measurement window, the feature-dependent mean and variance relationships are nonlinear or high dimensional, and credible external instruments are unavailable. In contrast to separate flexible regressions, which may reproduce reduced-form predictive associations, the proposed approach uses conditional second-moment variation to distinguish the two structural interaction directions while allowing neural networks to approx-imate complex nuisance functions. Its practical value lies not only in producing interaction estimates but also in providing diagnostics for the strength of the heteroscedastic identifying variation, remaining conditional residual dependence, variance calibration, gradient concentra-tion, and sensitivity to nonconvex optimization. Potential applications include price–quantity adjustment, interactions between users and online platforms, and coupled biological, neural, or engineering processes, provided that the covariates are predetermined and the maintained structural invariance and conditional shock-orthogonality assumptions are substantively credi-ble. The method should thus be viewed as a complement to instrumental-variable and acyclic causal-learning approaches for feedback systems in which identification through heterogeneous conditional variances is empirically plausible. The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 introduces the neural simultaneous-equation model and the quasi-likelihood objective and discusses identification of bidirectional interactions. Sec-tion 3 explains the learning algorithm. Section 4 presents Monte Carlo evidence. Section 5 presents an empirical illustration, and Section 6 concludes.

# 2.1 Structural model and causal interpretation 

We observe {y1i, y 2i, z i}ni=1 , where y1i and y2i are the two outcomes jointly determined within the focal outcome window, and zi denotes the raw information available before that window, as well as time-invariant attributes determined independently of the within-window outcomes. Our primary statistical target is the pair of directed structural interactions between y1 and y2.Under Assumption 1, these parameters also represent direct causal interactions. Let Tpre denote a preprocessing map fitted exclusively on the training sample, and define the model input by xi = Tpre (zi). We refer to xi as the vector of predetermined covariates. For-mally, each component of zi must satisfy at least one of the following timing conditions: (i) it is realized strictly before the focal outcome window; (ii) it is time invariant over the sample and is not constructed from focal-window outcomes or decisions; or (iii) it is a deterministic trans-formation of variables satisfying (i) or (ii), where all data-dependent transformation parameters are estimated from the training sample only. Variables measured or constructed using y1i, y2i, or other variables jointly determined within the focal outcome window are excluded from zi. In particular, a variable is not treated as pre-determined merely because it is observed in the data; its construction must not use contempo-raneous outcomes, contemporaneous residuals, or post-outcome information. We parameterize the unknown functions using feedforward neural networks. For each k =1, 2, the mean network fk(·; νk) maps xi to a scalar output. It consists of fully connected lay-ers with nonlinear activation functions, such as the Gaussian error linear unit (GELU), tanh ,5sigmoid, and softplus, followed by a linear output layer. The parameter vector νk collects all weights and biases in this mean network. This specification allows the feature effects in the structural mean equations to be highly nonlinear while keeping γ as a low-dimensional and interpretable causal target. Similarly, egk(·; αk) is a scalar-valued neural network for the unconstrained variance index. The variance network captures how the magnitude of the unobserved structural shock changes across the feature space. This conditional heteroscedasticity is not treated as a nuisance artifact; rather, it provides identifying information for separating the two directed interactions in the simultaneous system. The four neural networks, f1, f 2, eg1, eg2, are trained jointly with the structural interaction parameters γ1 and γ2. Joint training is necessary because the residuals 

e1i = y1i − γ1y2i − f1(xi; ν1), (6) 

e2i = y2i − γ2y1i − f2(xi; ν2), (7) depend on both the structural interaction parameters and the neural mean functions. The vari-ance networks then weight these residuals through the diagonal Gaussian quasi-likelihood. The resulting estimator therefore learns nonlinear feature effects, conditional noise levels, and bidi-rectional causal interactions in a single optimization problem. # 3 Learning algorithm 

# 3.1 Training challenges 

The identification results in Section 2 describe the population source of identification for bidi-rectional interactions. In finite samples, however, the proposed model must be trained as a heteroscedastic neural regression system. This creates a nontrivial optimization problem. The model contains neural networks for both the conditional mean functions f1, f 2 and the condi-tional variance functions g1, g 2. Consequently, variation in the observed outcomes can be ex-plained in two different ways: by changing the fitted structural mean, or by changing the fitted conditional variance. This mean–variance allocation problem is intrinsic to likelihood-based heteroscedastic neural learning. Recent work on heteroscedastic neural regression has shown that maximum-likelihood train-ing of mean and variance networks can produce undesirable behavior [3, 6, 13, 15]. In particular, a flexible variance network may compensate for errors in the mean network, thereby degrading the mean-function fit. Conversely, an overly flexible mean network may absorb variation that should be represented as conditional noise. The resulting variance estimates may be miscal-ibrated, and the likelihood can favor solutions that are numerically favorable but statistically misleading. These issues are especially relevant in the present setting because the variance func-tions do more than estimate uncertainty; they also provide identifying variation for the causal interaction parameters. 13 The difficulty is amplified by simultaneity. In a standard heteroscedastic regression prob-lem, the target is usually a conditional mean or a predictive distribution. In the present model, the main targets are the structural interaction parameters γ1 and γ2. The residuals used in the likelihood are (6) and (7). Thus, errors in the mean networks, variance networks, or interac-tion parameters all affect the same likelihood terms. A naive neural predictor can easily learn the association between y1 and y2, but such an association does not by itself distinguish struc-tural feedback from reduced-form dependence. The role of the heteroscedastic likelihood is to distinguish structural feedback from reduced-form dependence by searching for interaction parameters that make the structural residuals conditionally diagonal. This separation can be fragile when the nuisance networks overfit. Another practical issue is variance collapse. Since the diagonal Gaussian quasi-likelihood contains the terms log gk(xi; αk) and e2

> ki

/g k(xi; αk), very small fitted variances can dominate the objective. If a variance network assigns an artificially small variance to observations with small residuals, the likelihood may improve without producing a reliable representation of the conditional noise process. This behavior can destabilize gradient-based optimization and con-taminate the estimation of γ. For this reason, the variance functions are parameterized using a softplus link and a positive lower bound, as described in Section 2.1. The model is also sensitive to regularization. The regularization strength on the mean net-works controls how much outcome variation is absorbed by f1 and f2, whereas the regularization strength on the variance networks controls how flexible the heteroscedastic identifying signal is allowed to be. If the variance networks are too restricted, the model may fail to exploit the nonproportional variation in the conditional variances required for identification. If they are too flexible, the fitted variances may overfit idiosyncratic residual patterns. Similarly, overly flexi-ble mean networks can reduce the residual variation from which heteroscedastic identification is obtained. Hence the regularization parameters directly affect the practical balance between nuisance-function learning and structural-parameter estimation. These considerations motivate the training strategy developed below. Section 3.2 introduces a stabilized penalized quasi-likelihood objective, bounded parameterization of the interaction coefficients, validation-based hyperparameter selection, and early stopping. Section 3.3 then de-scribes diagnostics designed to check whether the fitted model contains enough heteroscedastic variation to support identification and whether the selected objective provides informative local evidence about the interaction parameters.

# 4 Simulation study 

# 4.1 Monte Carlo design 

We evaluate the proposed estimator in a simulation environment designed to reflect the main difficulties of heteroscedastic neural causal learning: bidirectional feedback, nonlinear nuisance functions, feature-dependent structural noise, irrelevant covariates, and non-Gaussian shocks. Because there is no standard benchmark design for this problem, we construct the design to make the identifying heteroscedastic signal explicit while remaining flexible enough to test neural-network estimation. For each Monte Carlo replication, data are generated from the simultaneous structural system (1)–(2). The true bidirectional interaction parameters are fixed at (γ1, γ 2) = (0 .5, 0.4) , so that 

1 − γ1γ2 = 0 .8. Thus the system contains nonnegligible feedback in both directions while remaining away from the singular boundary. The observed outcomes are obtained by solving this two-equation system for (y1i, y 2i). The covariate vector xi = ( xi1, . . . , x iJ )⊤ is generated from a Gaussian copula with correlated coordinates and bounded marginal support on (0 , 1) .We fix the feature dimension at J = 100 .The structural mean functions f1 and f2 and the variance-index functions eg1 and eg2 are non-linear functions of a subset of the covariates. The conditional variance functions are specified as (5) with gmin = 0 .01 . The functional forms are taken from [1] and include interactions among the covariates. The mean functions use coordinates up to xi7, and the variance-index functions use coordinates up to xi, 10 ; the remaining coordinates are irrelevant. This allows us to examine whether the estimator can recover the structural interaction parameters when many 18 observed features are available but only a subset contains useful signal. The exact formulas for the covariate generation, nonlinear benchmark functions, and mean and variance functions are reported in Appendix C. We use three sample sizes: n ∈ { 5000 , 10000 , 20000 }. For each replication, the scale of the structural mean functions is adjusted so that the empirical signal-to-noise ratio in each structural equation is set equal to R ∈ { 4, 1} by construction. The case R = 4 represents a relatively favorable setting with a strong structural mean signal, whereas R = 1 represents a noisier setting in which accurate use of heteroscedastic variation and regularization becomes more important. The standardized shocks are generated from centered and scaled chi-squared random vari-ables. Hence the structural shocks are non-Gaussian, while their conditional mean and variance follow the model specification. This makes the diagonal Gaussian objective a quasi-likelihood rather than a correctly specified full likelihood. The same data-generating process is used for all estimators. The parameter β in the SEM-β-NLL procedures is therefore not part of the data-generating process; it is a training-stabilization parameter used only in estimation. This distinction is important because the Monte Carlo design evaluates whether stabilized heteroscedastic neural training improves recovery of the structural interaction parameters under the same underlying simultaneous-equation system.

# B Details of the diagnostics 

The proposed estimator uses conditional heteroscedasticity to distinguish bidirectional structural interactions from reduced-form association. A fitted model may achieve a favorable validation quasi-likelihood while providing little information about γ, particularly when the fitted conditional variances are nearly proportional, the fitted structural residuals remain conditionally dependent, or inverse-variance weighting concentrates residual-driven gradients on a small subset of observations. Model parameters and the training epoch are selected using the original unpenalized VNQLL rather than the 

β-weighted training criterion. After selection, the diagnostics are evaluated once on a held-out diagnostic sam-ple. This separation limits mechanical overfitting of the diagnostic moments and places fitted specifications on a common quasi-log-likelihood scale. The diagnostics are descriptive model checks, not selection criteria or formal specification tests.

Regularization coefficients are selected by validation-based quasi-random search. Candidate configurations are generated using a scrambled Sobol sequence on the unit hypercube and then linearly transformed to the relevant log-penalty search space. The scrambling seed is set as a deterministic function of the design point, replication, and estimator, so that the hyperparameter search is reproducible while remaining independent across replications. The Sobol design is used because it gives more uniform coverage of the low-dimensional regularization space than ordinary pseudo-random search for a fixed number of candidate configurations. The number of first-stage Sobol candidates depends on the dimension of the regularization search. Four-dimensional SEM specifications use 32 first-stage candidates, whereas two-dimensional SEM specifications use 20 first-stage candidates. Thus, the main untied SEM-DNN, SEM-PAB, and SEM-Kernel specifications use 32 first-stage candidates. The two-dimensional SEM ablations, including no-variance regularization, no-mean regu-larization, and tied regularization, use 20 first-stage candidates. The staged continuation protocol is otherwise unchanged. In the first stage, all Sobol configurations are trained from initialization with a maximum budget of 30 epochs, and the 8 configurations with the most favorable VNQLL value are promoted. For each promoted configuration, the saved weights and optimizer state are restored from the epoch within that stage at which the validation criterion was minimized, not necessarily from the terminal epoch of the stage. In the second stage, the promoted configurations are continued from their restored stage-best checkpoints and trained through at most epoch 100 , and the best 3 configurations are promoted using the same validation-based rule. In the third stage, these 3 configurations are continued from their restored stage-best checkpoints with a budget up to epoch 200, and the best configuration is selected. The selected configuration is then restored to its best validation checkpoint from the third stage and continued until at most epoch 2000. The reported estimate is taken from the checkpoint that attains the smallest original validation criterion L(0) val (θ) over the entire continuation path. For the Single-DNN benchmark, hyperparameter optimization is conducted separately for the two equations. Each equation uses a one-dimensional log-penalty search over 

log 10 ϕν ∈ [−8, −1] ,

# D Ablation studies 

D.1 Alternative variance structure 

We modify the data-generating process so that the two structural variance functions become increasingly correlated and, ultimately, proportional. The baseline design uses nonproportional conditional variances g1(xi) and g2(xi),which satisfy the identifying condition in Lemma 1. This ablation weakens that source of identifying variation while holding the remaining features of the design as close as possible to the baseline. Specifically, after computing the baseline variance functions g1(xi) and g2(xi), we define 

gprop 1 (xi) = g1(xi), gprop 2 (xi) = (1 − c)g2(xi) + cg 1(xi), (D.1) where c ∈ { 0, 0.5, 1} controls the degree to which the second variance function is moved toward the first. When 

c = 0 , the baseline nonproportional design is recovered. When c = 1 , the two variance functions are identical, so the variance ratio is constant and the point-identification condition in Lemma 1 fails. All other structural functional forms are held fixed; shocks are regenerated and signal-to-noise ratio normal-izations are recomputed under the modified variances. While the standardized shocks uki are kept unchanged, the structural shocks εki are regenerated using gprop 1 (xi) and gprop 2 (xi). The mean-function scaling constants s1 and 

s2 are then recomputed using the proportional variances so that the target empirical signal-to-noise ratio remains 

R in each structural equation. For all ablation studies, including this one, we fix the sample size at n = 10000 .Table D.1 shows that making the conditional variances closer to proportional redistributes, rather than uni-formly increases, estimation error across the two interaction coefficients. As c increases, the root mean squared error (RMSE) of ̂ γ1 decreases, whereas that of ̂ γ2 rises sharply; under exact proportionality ( c = 1 ), the latter reaches 0.345 for R = 4 and 0.248 for R = 1 . Thus, when the variance ratio becomes constant and point identifi-cation fails, the fitted criterion may still estimate one direction relatively accurately, but it cannot reliably separate both structural effects, with the remaining ambiguity absorbed asymmetrically across the two coefficients.

To capture persistence and intermittency in movement, the model also includes: 1. the fraction of movement-valid historical weeks with positive movement; 2. the fraction with zero movement; 3. movement in the most recent movement-valid historical week; 4. mean movement during the first half of the historical window; 5. mean movement during the second half of the historical window; and 6. the difference between the second-half and first-half means. The half-window summaries are included only when each half contains at least the prespecified minimum number of movement-valid weeks. Otherwise, the corresponding value is coded as missing. The variable SALE is not treated as a binary indicator. For each sale code appearing in the training-store historical data, a separate historical frequency is constructed as the number of movement-valid historical weeks carrying that code divided by N H,M sj . Rare codes are pooled according to the training-only categorical pooling rule in Section E.8. A separate frequency records weeks for which SALE is blank or unavailable. No interpretation is imposed that all nonblank sale codes represent an identical form of promotion. Historical price summaries are computed only over HPsj . They include the mean and standard deviation of log package price, the most recent available historical log package price, and the difference between the second-half and first-half mean log prices when both quantities can be constructed. For each store-week (s, t ), define the reference price as the median log price per ounce among cereal UPCs having a price-valid observation in that store and week. The relative log price for UPC j is the difference between its log price per ounce and this store-week median. The historical mean and standard deviation of relative log price are then computed over the weeks in HPsj for which the store-week median is available. This relative-price variable measures the UPC’s historical price position within the cereal category at the same store and week. It is not constructed for weeks without a usable UPC price, and missing relative prices are not replaced with zero. 

Store-level variables. Historical cereal-category movement for store s is defined as the average weekly category total over the historical window. Specifically, weekly category movement is first summed over UPCs within each store and week, and the resulting totals are then averaged over the 22 historical weeks. Weeks with no movement-valid cereal records are retained as missing rather than assigned zero. The model includes the logarithm of one plus historical average weekly category movement. It also includes the fraction of historical weeks for which the store has at least one movement-valid cereal record and the mean number of movement-valid cereal UPCs observed per historical week. 33 The following time-invariant store variables are taken directly from the demographic file when a unique linkage by the store identifier is available: 1. pricing zone; 2. store cluster; 3. urban indicator; 4. average weekly store volume; 5. latitude and longitude; and 6. a prespecified set of neighborhood demographic characteristics. E.9 Sample splitting, model selection, and seedwise reporting 

Because observations sharing a store are exposed to common local demand, pricing policies, product availability, and store characteristics, the baseline split is performed at the store level. Every store–UPC observation inherits the partition assignment of its store. No store appears in more than one partition. The empirical objective gives equal weight to retained store–UPC observations. Consequently, stores carrying more retained UPCs and UPCs appearing in more retained stores receive greater aggregate weight. The baseline analysis does not reweight obser-vations to give equal total weight to each store or UPC. Alternative weighting schemes are considered separately in Section E.10. 

Deterministic store-blocked partition. Let S0 denote the set of stores appearing in the preliminarily eligible sample for the selected focal window. This set is determined using only the window, identifier-linkage, and prelimi-nary row-availability rules in Section E.3. It is defined before applying training-derived price screens, UPC-support rules, or preprocessing transformations. For each store s ∈ S 0, construct a canonical store string by converting the store identifier to its base-10 rep-resentation without leading or trailing whitespace. Apply the xxHash64 algorithm to the UTF-8 encoding of the concatenation of a published integer seed, a fixed separator, and the canonical store string. Interpret the resulting hash as an unsigned 64-bit integer. Stores are sorted in increasing order of their hash values. Hash ties, if any, are resolved by increasing numeric store identifier. Let nS = |S 0|, nS, train = ⌊0.60 nS ⌋, and nS, val = ⌊0.20 nS ⌋. The first nS, train stores in the ranked list are assigned to the training partition, the next nS, val stores to the validation partition, and the remaining stores to the diagnostic partition. This ranked-hash procedure produces a deterministic allocation of approximately 60%, 20%, and 20% while avoiding potentially small partitions that can arise from applying fixed hash intervals to a modest number of stores. The hash algorithm, seed, separator, encoding, canonicalization rule, and tie-breaking rule are reported in the replication materials. The partition is computed once. It is not modified in response to final sample sizes, focal outcomes, estimated coefficients, validation losses, convergence behavior, or diagnostic results. After stores have been assigned, all training-derived sample-construction and preprocessing objects are fitted using training stores only. These objects include: 38 1. price-per-ounce screening bounds; 2. the UPC-support set; 3. continuous-variable imputation values; 4. clipping thresholds and standardization constants; 5. categorical pooling maps; 6. the product-description vocabulary and inverse-document-frequency weights; 7. text singular-vector loadings; and 8. diagnostic-feature transformations. The fitted objects are then applied unchanged to observations from validation and diagnostic stores. Eligibility conditions that depend on these objects are evaluated separately in each partition.

Pair-random benchmark. As a deliberately less stringent predictive benchmark, store–UPC pairs are assigned directly to training, validation, and diagnostic partitions using the deterministic ranked-hash procedure and the same approximate 60%, 20%, and 20% proportions. Under this split, a store or UPC may appear in more than one partition. The resulting validation and diagnostic observations may therefore share store-level or product-level information with the training data. All training-derived objects are re-estimated using the pair-random training par-tition. The pair-random results are not treated as an alternative primary design because cross-partition dependence can make predictive performance and residual diagnostics appear more favorable. To assess sensitivity to the selected market period, the complete empirical pipeline is repeated for prespecified alternative starting weeks in the admissible candidate set Wcand . The alternative win-dows are selected by a calendar rule fixed before model estimation. The robustness analysis uses the first two nonoverlapping admissible focal windows after the baseline window.
