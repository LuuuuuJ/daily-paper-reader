# When Does Real-World Data Make a Randomized Trial More Efficient, and How Would You Know? A Report Card, an Efficiency Map, and Selection-Aware Inference for Adaptive-TMLE 

M. Ehsan Karim ∗

School of Population and Public Health, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada, and Centre for Advancing Health Outcomes, St. Paul’s Hospital, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada 

Abstract 

Augmenting a randomized controlled trial with real-world data promises greater efficiency, but how much a given fusion actually delivers, and how to attach honest uncertainty to that gain, is rarely characterized. Using adaptive targeted maximum likelihood estimation (A-TMLE) as the running example, we develop three reproducible tools for honest evidence from combined trial and real-world data. First, a report card that makes the estimator’s data-adaptively learned bias model auditable, measuring how well it recovers the true enrollment-effect surface and attributing the estimator’s variance to its structural parts. Second, a map of when fusion helps versus hurts, benchmarked against an efficient trial-only estimator: the gain is driven primarily by the magnitude of the real-world bias rather than its functional complexity, a dominance an exact variance identity explains; it crosses break-even near a moderate bias and erodes as the trial grows, so the advantage is finite-sample rather than a form of super-efficiency. Third, selection-aware inference for the gain, treated as a data-adaptive estimand: the naive standard error undercovers, and among ten candidate standard errors only a block jackknife is calibrated, though conservatively so. Three openly available fusions, in a biomedical HIV trial, a public-health trial, and a job-training trial, span the map and show the difference an honest interval makes for real-world evidence. 

Key words: Adaptive debiased machine learning; Cross-fitting; Data fusion; Efficient influence function; Highly adaptive lasso; Jackknife; Real-world evidence; Reliability of machine-learning methods; Selection-aware inference; Targeted maximum likelihood estimation.

Background and motivation. A large literature now combines the two — by reweighting for representativeness, by test-then-pool borrowing, or by shrinkage — and a recurring promise is that fusion “buys” efficiency over using the trial alone [Colnet et al., 2024]. Two questions, however, are seldom answered for any given method: how much efficiency does the fusion actually deliver in finite samples, and how does one form an honest confidence statement about that gain? Adaptive-TMLE (A-TMLE) of van der Laan et al. [2026] — a member of the targeted maximum likelihood estimation framework [van der Laan and Rubin, 2006] — is an attractive entry into this space and our running example throughout. It targets the trial-respecting ATE under only the assumptions known to hold for an RCT plus a trial-enrollment positivity condition, and it integrates RWD not through an exchange-ability assumption but through a learned bias model : the ATE is written as a pooled-ATE estimand minus a bias estimand — the conditional effect of trial enrollment on the outcome — estimated by a data-adaptively selected, relaxed highly-adaptive-lasso (HAL) working model. The method is √n-consistent, asymptotically normal, and is argued to be finite-sample super-efficient, with its efficiency gain held to be driven by the complexity , not the magnitude , of the bias, so that “even if the bias is large in magnitude, provided it is a simple function . . . A-TMLE is still expected to yield efficiency gain” [van der Laan et al., 2026]. 

The two gaps. First, the learned object is invisible . The bias working model τS — the very object the theory says drives the gain — is never reported, plotted, or audited; a reader sees only a final estimate and a confidence interval. This is true not only of A-TMLE but of the broader family of adaptive/debiased machine-learning estimators that build and then debias a data-driven working model [van der Laan et al., 2023, Hubbard et al., 2016]: the working model is treated as an inferential nuisance, never as a reportable artifact. Second, the gain is characterized only qualitatively . The “complexity not magnitude” claim is asymptotic and oracle-conditional, and the authors themselves flag the gap: “it remains unclear in practice how much efficiency gain one should expect . . . [in] some [cases] the efficiency gain is small” [van der Laan et al., 2026]. There is, moreover, no standard error for the efficiency gain itself — it is reported only as a Monte Carlo ratio across simulation runs. 

Contributions. We close both gaps with three contributions — together, a practitioner’s toolkit for honest evidence from combined trial and real-world data — developed and demonstrated on A-TMLE as the running example (their extension to the broader adaptive-debiased family is a conjecture, not a claim; Table 1). (1) A report card for a data-adaptively learned working model : we audit a fitted estimator to make the learned bias object legible — measuring how well it recovers the true enrollment-effect surface, attributing the estimator’s influence-curve variance to its structural components, and flagging over-sparse selection. The closest prior work is the adaptive-debiased-ML framework of van der Laan et al. [2023], which formalizes the oracle projection target and its efficiency bound; our delta is to audit the realized working model rather than only debias it . (2) A practical characterization of when the fusion’s finite-sample variance gain materializes : we map the gain as a joint function of bias magnitude, complexity, the reference distribution, and n. The closest prior work is the elastic integrative analysis of Yang et al. [2023], which decides whether to borrow via one compatibility test; our delta is to characterize when and how much of that variance gain materializes, and to explain its magnitude-dominance through an exact population-oracle variance identity (Section 4.4) . (3) Selection-aware inference for the efficiency gain : treating the gain as a data-adaptive estimand, we show standard cross-fitting, CV-TMLE, and an assumption-lean HulC interval all fail, while a block jackknife is conservatively valid; a heuristic account (Section 4.5) explains why. Positioning. This paper sits at the intersection of RCT+RWD fusion [Colnet et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2023, Yang and Ding, 2020, Rosenman et al., 2023, Viele et al., 2014, Dang et al., 2025], adaptive/debiased machine learning with data-adaptive target parameters [van der Laan et al., 2023, Hubbard et al., 2016, Li et al., 2025], and selection-aware inference and resampling [Berk et al., 2013, Chernozhukov et al., 2018, Zheng and van der Laan, 2011, Kuchibhotla et al., 2024, Efron and Stein, 1981, Quenouille, 1956]. The dominant fusion paradigms are test-then-pool, shrinkage, and Bayesian dynamic borrowing [Ibrahim and Chen, 2000, Schmidli et al., 2014, Pocock, 1976], which borrow all-or-nothing or via a global weight; A-TMLE instead decomposes the ATE and borrows direction-by-direction through an explicitly learned bias function — but, like the rest of this lineage, never reports it. The closest selection-aware fusion method, experiment-selector CV-TMLE [Dang et al., 2025], data-adaptively selects between the trial-only and pooled estimands on each cross-validation fold, yet reports no honest standard error for the resulting efficiency gain and never audits the learned bias object — the two gaps our contributions target. More broadly, the standard selection-aware toolbox (cross-fitting, CV-TMLE, HulC) assumes a fixed target functional and does not transfer to a data-adaptive efficiency estimand. Two themes central to real-world evidence run through all three contributions: the learned bias model τS is precisely the conditional enrollment-effect surface that a transportability analysis between the trial and the external population would need to model [Stuart et al., 2011, Westreich et al., 2017, Dahabreh et al., 2020], so the report card doubles as a diagnostic for how the two populations differ; and the selection-aware standard error supplies uncertainty quantification for the efficiency gain, a quantity otherwise reported without one. Because that bias model is itself a data-adaptively learned, machine-learning object, auditing it is a concrete instance of the reliability of AI/ML methods for real-world evidence — the report card turns an otherwise-opaque learned component into an inspectable one. The extended treatment, delineating each contribution from its closest prior work, is in Web Appendix B. 

Aims and roadmap. We stress throughout that an efficiency comparison is reference-dependent :our claims are relative to an efficient RCT-only estimator. Framed honestly, the work is a rigorous, reproducible characterization of when and how much fusion helps, and of how to report it — not a 3M. E. Karim Efficiency of RCT +RWD fusion with A-TMLE

3.2 A report card for the learned working model 

We attach three diagnostics to a fitted A-TMLE fusion estimator that make the learned bias object legible. Recovery compares the targeted learned surface bτS (W, A ) to the truth τS, 0(W, A ) on known data-generating processes, via cor ⋆ = cor( bτS , τ S, 0). Attribution decomposes the influence-curve variance var( D) into the pooled-projection variance var( DA) and the variance the bias-correction step adds or removes, reported alongside the gain (6). Drift is a Π-weighted distance between the cross-validation-selected and the TMLE-targeted τS , a flag for an over-sparse working model. On real data, where τS, 0 is unknown, the report card instead reports the learned model’s effective dimension and the variance attribution (Section 5). 

3.3 Selection-aware standard errors 

The efficiency gain (6) is itself an estimand, and its naive influence-function SE conditions on the one cross-validation-selected HAL working model. We benchmark ten SE estimators of log(gain): the naive influence-function SE; a delete-a-fold block jackknife that re-selects the working model on each S-stratified leave-fold-out subsample; a cross-fitted out-of-fold influence-curve variance (with model selection refit within each training fold); three ridge stabilizations of the cross-fit information-matrix inverse ( λ = 0 .01 , 0.05 , 0.2); a winsorized cross-fit; a principled CV-TMLE 

that re-targets a one-dimensional fluctuation for each of τA and τS on each held-out fold; CV-TMLE plus ridge; and an assumption-lean HulC interval (the convex hull of six disjoint-group gains, valid under only median-unbiasedness). 4.1 Design 

We generate RCT+RWD data with a true, homogeneous ATE of 1 .5 and a bias that enters only the RWD arm. With three standard-normal covariates and Gaussian outcome noise, the struc-tural outcome is Y = 2 .5 + 0 .9W1 + 1 .1W2 + 2 .7W3 + 1 .5A + UY + 1{S=0 } B, so the treatment effect is the constant 1 .5 in every cell and the bias B = B(W, A ) perturbs only the external arm. Treatment is randomized in the trial ( P (A=1 | S=1) = 0 .67) and confounded externally (P (A=1 | S=0 , W ) = expit(0 .5W1)), matching the original paper’s allocation. 4.2 The bias-model report card 

Table 3 reports recovery and the mean working-model size across the grid ( B = 1000 replicates per cell; Monte Carlo SE of cor ⋆ ≤ 0.005). Recovery is strong where it matters: cor ⋆ ≥ 0.82 for every complexity once the bias reaches two residual SDs ( m ≥ 2), and ≈ 0.95 for a linear bias, degrading only at small magnitude where the true τS is near-constant (and the gain is already ∼ 1.15, so the auditor needs the tool least); it is undefined at m = 0, where there is no bias to recover. On the original paper’s own scenarios (a)/(b) the targeted surface attains cor ⋆ = 0 .931/0 .949 (each over 

B = 8 diagnostic replicates, Monte Carlo SD ≈ 0.02), and it reproduces qualitative structure — for instance that the W1-dependence of the bias is present in the control arm and absent in the treated arm (Figure 2). Because cor ⋆ is invariant to affine rescaling, we separately certify the scale 

of bτS : it enters Ψ # multiplicatively, so a shape-correct but scale-attenuated surface would bias the point estimate — yet A-TMLE’s relative bias stays ≤ 4.6% throughout (Table 5), so the scale is certified implicitly. Attribution shows var( DA) inflates roughly six- to seven-and-a-half-fold across the grid (from ≈ 4.2 at m = 0 to ≈ 25–31 at m = 4, by complexity) — pooling a noisier, biased outcome, not the bias-correction step itself, is the dominant cost (Web Appendix D). Drift is small in the linear DGPs and grows monotonically with bias (from ≈ 0.002 at m = 0 to ≈ 0.03–0 .05 at 

m = 4), a quantitative warning that the working model is being stretched. The report card thus makes auditable the central data-adaptive object the original method leaves unreported. 

4.3 The efficiency map 

Table 4 reports the gain (6) over magnitude × complexity ( B = 1000, 15 ,000 fits, zero failures). Three findings. 1. Magnitude is the dominant axis. The gain falls monotonically with m, crossing 1 .0 just above m ≈ 1 (between m = 1 and m = 2) (a bias of ∼one outcome-residual SD) for every complexity. The mechanism is transparent: as noted in Section 4.2, the pooled-projection variance var( DA) inflates ∼ 6–7 .5× across the range — pooling a noisier biased outcome is the cost, not the bias-correction step. 2. Complexity is a second-order amplifier. At m ≤ 1 the three complexity curves agree to within ∼ 0.03; at m ≥ 2 they fan out (linear > interaction > wiggly), the spread growing from ∼ 0.17 ( m = 2) to ∼ 0.25 ( m = 4). Why the cross-fit family fails. The cross-fitted out-of-fold variance was the theoretically-motivated refinement; it does the opposite. Its fixed-truth coverage collapses to 0 .15–0 .52 (the self-centered 0 .60–0 .88 had merely hidden this) because its gain point estimate is badly biased: cross-fit underestimates the gain by up to 65% and the ridge variants swing from −41% to +130%, so these are poor estimators of the gain, not merely mis-calibrated SEs. The principled CV-TMLE also fails. The comparator the theory points to — a CV-TMLE that re-targets the working-model fluctuation on each held-out fold (one fluctuation each for τA

and τS , centering the validation score plain cross-fit leaves uncentered, in the spirit of the original paper’s Appendix-C construction) — also fails: fixed-truth coverage 0 .18–0 .51, gain biased −8 to 

−56%, no better than plain cross-fit, and ridge-stabilizing it only reintroduces overshoot. We are careful not to over-claim the mechanism : re-targeting is not inert (once a bias is present CV-TMLE is consistently less biased than plain cross-fit), and the τS information matrix is severely ill-conditioned and worsens with m — but the re-targeting fluctuation tracks the bias magnitude rather than the inverse conditioning, and both estimators are already ∼ 8–9% biased at m = 0 where conditioning is mildest, so we do not isolate conditioning as the sole driver. The honest summary is empirical: the out-of-fold reconstruction is both biased and high-variance. The full re-targeting and conditioning diagnostics (condition numbers, fluctuation magnitudes) are in Web Appendix D. 

HulC is far too wide and upward-biased. The assumption-lean HulC interval looks self-calibrated (own-mean coverage 0 .84–0 .90) but its point estimate is inflated 3 .5–5 .6×: its convex-hull guarantee needs each disjoint-subgroup gain to be median-unbiased, and on these small subsamples the per-group ratio is badly biased so the hull’s center explodes — we are applying HulC outside its design regime , and against the fixed truth it covers only 0 .07–0 .57. Why the naive and cross-fit standard errors fail: a heuristic, not a theorem. We give no 

theorem for the selection-aware standard error of the gain R = var( Drct )/var( Datmle ): it is built on a non-smooth CV-selection step, and a rigorous treatment would need stable-selection, eigenvalue, and differentiability conditions we do not impose. Under an oracle, stably-selected working model treated as an idealization, R is a smooth functional of two second moments of learned influence curves, and log R has influence function U + Γ with a plug-in part U and a working-model part Γ induced by the selection. This heuristic explains the three empirical findings. (i) The naive plug-in estimates the influence-curve variance by Var( U ), omitting Γ — not merely conditioning on the selected model; whether the omission widens or narrows coverage is not sign-determined in general, and empirically it undercovers (0 .70–0 .87). (ii) The plain cross-fit evaluates the bias-model influence curve on an out-of-fold score it does not re-center, so the uncentered out-of-fold second moment carries a nonnegative excess. This is a mechanism for the observed downward bias in 

bR — verified in sign and in n-scaling, and corroborated in trend by the instrumented out-of-fold reconstruction, whose uncentered mean grows with the bias though it is not pinned to a closed-form constant (Web Appendix G) — not a sign theorem, since the total also involves the DA block, the cross term, and a Π ⋆-replay bias nonzero at m=0. Under the oracle idealization above this excess shrinks with n, though the cross-fit’s coverage does not recover by n=5000 (Web Appendix E); empirically the failure is not governed by information-matrix conditioning. (iii) The delete-a-fold block jackknife, which re-selects and re-targets on each subsample, avoids the cross-fit’s out-of-fold defects and is empirically the best-covering candidate: at-or-above nominal fixed-truth coverage in all but one of the 40 cells (Web Appendix E), dipping to 0 .910 only at n=5000, wiggly, m=0 (where the gain has eroded to ≈ 1.01) and even there far ahead of every rival (0 .910 versus ≤ 0.54), doing so conservatively (1 .6–2 .2× over-wide). We do not claim it is consistent — the statistic is 18 M. E. Karim Efficiency of RCT +RWD fusion with A-TMLE 

non-smooth — nor super-efficient (the variance advantage erodes to one as n grows). The oracle idealization, assumptions, and caveats are in Web Appendix G.

Report gain/attribution CIs with the block jackknife : across 15 cells and 4 robustness slices it is the only selection-aware SE that is approximately unbiased and attains nominal (here conservative, ∼ 1.6–2 .2× over-wide) coverage. Because it inherits the naive full-data point estimate, its job is to calibrate the interval width rather than to de-bias the gain. The naive IF-SE is unsafe; the cross-fitted out-of-fold variance, its ridge/winsor stabilizations, and the principled CV-TMLE re-targeting are all both biased and anti-conservative here, for the reason made precise above; the HulC interval is far too wide and upward-biased to use at these sample sizes. We do not claim the block jackknife is asymptotically exact — it is admittedly inconsistent for this non-smooth statistic and is here only empirically calibrated — only that it is the single selection-aware SE attaining at-or-above nominal coverage in all but one of the 40 fixed-truth cells (minimum 0 .910), and the best-covering method in that one. 

# 5 Real-world illustrations 

We illustrate all three contributions on three openly-available fusions, ordered to span the efficiency map’s verdict rather than to endorse fusion: a biomedical HIV trial where fusion most clearly helps (gain ≈ 1.2), a public-health cluster-randomized trial with a modest gain ( ≈ 1.10), and a job-training trial that lands at or below parity (0 .91/0 .99).

# 6 Discussion 

Contributions and implications. We gave three reproducible tools for fused RCT+RWD es-timation, using A-TMLE as the running example: a report card that makes a learned bias working model auditable; a magnitude × complexity × reference × n map of when the fusion’s finite-sample variance gain actually materializes; and a selection-aware standard error for the efficiency gain. Together they convert a qualitative promise (“fusion buys efficiency”) into an inspectable object, a quantitative map with a parity crossing near a bias of one residual SD, and an honest interval — and the real-data illustrations show the difference an honest interval makes. 

Practical recommendations. Report the report card alongside any A-TMLE (or adaptive-debiased) fusion estimate, so the learned bias object and its influence-curve variance attribution are visible rather than hidden. Read the efficiency gain as reference-relative and n-dependent : at realistic sample sizes it is “near break-even unless the RWD is both abundant and nearly unbi-ased.” Form gain/attribution intervals with the block jackknife — the naive influence-function SE undercovers and the cross-fit/CV-TMLE/HulC routes are both biased and anti-conservative. 

Limitations and future work. Our efficiency verdicts are relative to a matched, efficient (correctly-specified-GLM, cross-fitted) RCT-only estimator; Section 4.3.1 shows the sub-unity ver-dict is not an artifact of this choice (it holds against a flexible SuperLearner and relaxed-HAL), but the numerical gain is reference-relative. We benchmark against an RCT-only estimator, rather than against a selective-fusion competitor such as experiment-selector CV-TMLE [Dang et al., 2025], by design: the efficiency map asks what fusion buys relative to using the trial alone , so the trial-only estimator is the correct denominator, whereas a fusion competitor would answer a dif-ferent question. A further limitation is the provenance of our real-world external arms: two of the 24 M. E. Karim Efficiency of RCT +RWD fusion with A-TMLE 

three real-data fusions (WASH Benefits and ACTG175) use constructed , within-trial external arms rather than independently-collected observational data, while the third (LaLonde NSW with its PSID/CPS comparison groups), which does use genuine non-experimental controls, lands near par-ity — so the regime of large, genuinely unmeasured-confounded real-world data is exercised mainly in our simulations, and the real-data sections are best read as faithful end-to-end demonstrations of the toolkit rather than as evidence on the magnitude of genuine confounding. By contrast, the original A-TMLE analysis [van der Laan et al., 2026] augmented a cardiovascular-outcomes trial with a genuinely observational electronic-health-record cohort and reported a real efficiency gain in precisely this regime; our audit exercises that regime only in simulation, so pairing the report card and the selection-aware interval with such an observational fusion is a natural next step. The efficiency gain erodes with n, while the selection-aware verdict is confirmed not to be a small-sample artifact (it holds at n = 2000). The main DGPs assign trial membership determinis-tically, so Π( W ) is constant and enrollment positivity holds trivially; only the positivity slice and the W -dependent-enrollment map of Web Appendix E exercise a genuinely W -dependent Π( W ), and there the picture is mixed — we report it plainly. The selection-aware verdict is unchanged (the jackknife stays conservatively valid and cross-fit/CV-TMLE still collapse) and the gain-truth slice is if anything higher; but A-TMLE’s own ATE coverage degrades at the wiggly, large-bias corner, and the degradation worsens with n (the wiggly, large-bias under-coverage noted in Sec-tion 4.3; Web Appendix E). That is genuine under-coverage where the trial-enrollment positivity assumption is most stressed, not conservatism, and we flag it as such. The within-trial effect is also homogeneous by construction (constant CATE = 1 .5), so the map is a pure variance contrast and does not exercise A-TMLE’s heterogeneous-CATE learning. The jackknife is conservative, not exact (coverage 0 .98–1 .00, ratio ≈ 1.6–2 .2); an exact selection-aware SE [Lee et al., 2016, Fithian et al., 2014] remains open, and the cross-fit, ridge, winsor, and CV-TMLE routes were all built and shown not to calibrate, so CV-TMLE re-targeting is not that route. The most important extensions are a fuller W -dependent-enrollment map (the natural transportability extension, where the trial and external populations differ in covariate distribution, which would let the named identification assumption do real work rather than hold vacuously: the positivity slice suggests the efficiency ver-dict survives, but the coverage degradation above shows the assumption then does real work and must be respected), a finer attribution sub-decomposing DS into its W/ Π/β pieces via a projection representation that resolves the HAL basis collinearity [Li et al., 2025], and survival/coarsened-data endpoints. We conjecture the report card and the selection-aware-inference result extend to other adaptive-debiased estimators that build and then debias a learned working model; demonstrating this beyond A-TMLE is future work. 

Conclusion. Fused RCT+RWD estimators promise efficiency, but how much and how to report it honestly has been left implicit. Auditing the learned bias object, mapping when the gain mate-rializes, and using the calibrated block jackknife make the promise inspectable, quantitative, and honest — on simulated grids and on real biomedical, public-health, and job-training fusions.
