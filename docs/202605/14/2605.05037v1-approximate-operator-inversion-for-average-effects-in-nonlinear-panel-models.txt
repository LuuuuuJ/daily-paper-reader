# Approximate Operator Inversion for Average Effects in Nonlinear Panel Models ∗

## Jad Beyhum ‡ Geert Dhaene § Cavit Pakel ¶ Martin Weidner ‖

## May 7, 2026 

Abstract 

We study the estimation of average effects in nonlinear panel data models with fixed effects when the time dimension T is only moderately large. Our approach, called approximate operator inversion (AOI), offers a new perspective on bias cor-rection. Instead of first estimating unit-specific fixed effects and then correcting the resulting plug-in bias, AOI approximately inverts the likelihood-induced map-ping from the fixed-effect distribution to the outcome distribution. AOI can be interpreted as the limit of an infinitely iterated bias correction scheme, and this limit is available in closed form. We show that the bias of the AOI estimator has a rate double robustness property and converges to zero at an exponential rate in 

T under regularity conditions. Our asymptotic theory requires T → ∞ , but the exponential convergence rate of the bias means that finite-sample performance is very good even for moderately large T . We establish asymptotic normality and provide feasible inference. 

Keywords: Panel data, discrete choice, average effects, incidental parameters, ill-posed inverse problem

# 1 Introduction 

Nonlinear panel data models with fixed effects are central to empirical work in eco-nomics. In the standard semiparametric setting, the researcher specifies for each individ-ual unit i = 1 , . . . , n , a conditional probability (or density) f (Yi|Xi, A i; θ0) of outcomes 

Yi = ( Yi1, . . . , Y iT ) given the observed covariates Xi = ( Xi1, . . . , X iT ), the fixed effects 

Ai that capture unobserved heterogeneity, and the model parameter θ0. The conditional distribution of fixed effects π0(Ai|Xi), on the other hand, is left completely unspecified. Two objects of typical interest are the common model parameters θ0 and average effects of the form μ0 = E[μ(Xi, A i, θ 0)], such as average marginal effects or average treatment effects. The presence of unobserved fixed effects leads to the well-known incidental pa-rameter problem (Neyman and Scott, 1948), which complicates estimation of both θ0 and 

μ0. The literature frames the study of this problem along two asymptotic regimes, each with its own characteristic issues. (A) Large n and fixed T . In this setting, each unit contributes only a finite number of observations. Consequently, the distribution π0(Ai|Xi) can only be set-identified. Essentially, the issue at hand is inversion (or, lack thereof) of the likelihood-induced mapping from the fixed-effect distribution to the outcome distribution, as given by

More seriously, point-identification of μ0 largely fails. Except for some very specific cases, set identification is the norm rather than exception. (B) Large n and large T , with Ai consistently estimable. The analysis under this regime reasons that as T → ∞ each unit should accumulate enough information for 

Ai to be consistently estimable. This abstracts away from identification of π0(Ai|Xi)and allows for inference based on f (y | x, α ; θ) using plug-in consistent estimates of 

Ai. However, even though consistently estimable, the asymptotically growing number of fixed effects leads to the incidental parameter bias. The bias-correction literature offers various options for removing this asymptotic bias. Clearly, the large-T literature operates on the level of Ai and not π0(Ai|Xi), sidestep-2ping the fundamental inversion problem in (1). This poses an inherent limitation. Indeed, the large-T literature almost exclusively focuses on the correction of the leading O(1 /T )bias. While this is entirely justified for truly large values of T , in practice the remaining bias terms can potentially matter for moderate values of T , especially in more complicated models. At a practical level, complete correction of bias remains a virtually hopeless task. At a theoretical level, the focus on Ai rather than π0(Ai|Xi) limits the scope of traditional bias-correction methods. Motivated by these observations, we propose the novel approximate operator inversion (AOI) method. This method can be used to conduct inference on both θ0 and μ0 under large-n large-T asymptotics, though our focus in this paper will be on μ0. The AOI estimator operates directly on the level of π0(Ai|Xi) rather than of Ai. Specifically, our approach treats the estimation of μ0 as an operator inversion problem and it approximately inverts the likelihood-induced mapping from the fixed-effect distribution to the outcome distribution given in (1). By shifting the focus to the fundamental inversion problem, our approach bridges large-T analysis with the fixed-T literature. Crucially, although distinct from traditional bias-correction methods, the AOI estima-tor admits an iterated bias-correction interpretation and, in a well-defined sense, delivers infinite-order bias correction. This advances the state of the art in the large-T panel literature, where existing methods typically correct bias only up to a fixed, often first, order. The bias of AOI depends on two approximation components: how well the average-effect function μ(X, ·, θ 0) is approximated by a chosen basis, and how well the unknown fixed-effect distribution is approximated by another basis. The overall bias is governed by the product of these two approximation errors, a property we call rate double robustness .Rapid convergence in either component is enough for fast overall bias decay. Under regularity conditions, the bias decays at an exponential rate in T , i.e., O(ρT ) for some 0 < ρ < 1. This exponential decay property is a major improvement over the polynomial rates O(1 /T s) obtained in standard bias-correction methods. Operating on Ai rather than π0(Ai|Xi) can result in more than the loss of infinite-order bias-correction. We highlight this by studying a third asymptotic scenario that interpolates between the large-T and fixed-T settings. (C) Large n and large T , with Ai not consistently estimable. Although T → ∞ ,no unit is able to accumulate enough information on Ai: consistent estimation of 3Ai fails even as T tends to infinity. We show that θ0 and μ0 can nevertheless be consistently estimated in this regime under regularity conditions. The traditional large-T asymptotics implicitly assumes that all fixed effects are consis-tently estimable as long as T → ∞ . However, and crucially, consistent estimation of 

Ai is not a matter of whether T tends to infinity but of whether the Fisher information in data on Ai grows without bound as T → ∞ . Depending on the model and the co-variate design, Ai can fail to be consistently estimable, rendering existing bias-correction approaches non-operational. Our study of this scenario highlights that the extant bias-correction literature operates not under large-T asymptotics but a subset of it where Ai is consistently estimable. We believe that our paper is the first one to make this distinction. Importantly, under regularity conditions, AOI is inherently immune to this issue. The ideas in this paper apply to inference on both θ0 and μ0. From Section 2 onwards, however, we focus on estimation of μ0, which presents the greater challenge. Even in models where θ0 can be estimated consistently for fixed T (for example, using conditional likelihood methods), point identification of μ0 typically fails for fixed T because the dis-tribution of Ai is only partially identified from short panels. This motivates our focus on 

μ0 and the development of the AOI estimator for this purpose. 

Related literature. AOI builds on two prior contributions. The first is functional dif-ferencing (Bonhomme, 2012), which constructs moment conditions that identify θ0 while remaining exactly free of Ai, without any large-T approximation. Such exact moment conditions exist only in special models and for specific choices of μ0, and do not extend to average effects in general (see also Dano, 2023; Aguirregabiria and Carro, 2024, for ap-plications to average marginal effects). Bonhomme and Davezies (2017) adopt a similar inverse-problem perspective on average-effect estimation, but restrict attention to settings where μ0 is point-identified at fixed T , ruling out the discrete choice models considered here. The second is approximate functional differencing (Dhaene and Weidner, 2023), which replaces exact moment conditions with approximate ones whose error vanishes as T → ∞ ,broadening the scope to settings where exact moment conditions are unavailable. Both functional differencing and approximate functional differencing are general methods appli-cable to a broad class of nonlinear panel models. AOI adapts and extends this approach to the estimation of μ0, going beyond Dhaene and Weidner (2023) by developing the 4operator inversion perspective, establishing formal results on bias decay and asymptotic normality, and providing feasible inference procedures. The bounds literature offers a complementary approach, deriving sharp partial iden-tification regions under minimal assumptions (Honor´ e and Tamer, 2006; Chernozhukov et al., 2013; Davezies et al., 2021; Dobronyi et al., 2021; Pakel and Weidner, 2023; Boto-saru et al., 2024). In modern applications where n is large and T is only moderately large, these bounds can be very narrow, so that μ0 is nearly point-identified in practice. AOI and bounds approaches are therefore complementary. Bounds provide robust partial identification guarantees, while AOI provides point estimates and inference under large-T

approximations. More broadly, AOI contributes to the large literature on bias-correction in nonlin-ear panel models (Hahn and Newey, 2004; Arellano and Bonhomme, 2009; Dhaene and Jochmans, 2015; Higgins and Jochmans, 2024; Bonhomme et al., 2024). These methods rely on a preliminary consistent estimator of Ai. AOI does not require consistent esti-mation of Ai, making it applicable in a broader class of settings and delivering better finite-sample performance when T is small. 

Roadmap. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the model, defines the target average effect, and presents a motivating example. Section 3 formulates the estimation of average effects as an inversion problem and shows how AOI approximately solves it. Section 4 defines the AOI estimator and interprets it as an infinitely iterated bias-correction. Section 5 establishes the large-sample properties of the estimator, in-cluding the rate double robustness result, asymptotic normality, and feasible inference. Section 6 presents numerical results for a random-coefficient logit model. Section 7 dis-cusses inference on the common parameter θ0. Section 8 concludes. All proofs are collected in the Appendix.

# 3 Average effect estimation as an inversion problem 

The average effect μ0 = E[μ(X, A )] depends on the distribution of the unobserved A.We only observe outcomes Y , whose distribution is determined by the distribution of A

through the model. This section explains how to estimate μ0 by approximately inverting this relationship. The covariate value x ∈ X is fixed throughout and plays no essential role. Where it aids readability, we suppress x from the discussion. The model f (y | x, α ) tells us the probability of each outcome y given a particular fixed-effect value α. But we are not interested in f as a function of individual fixed effects. 10 What matters is the mapping that f induces at the level of distributions : if the fixed effect A has distribution π on A, then integrating f against π produces the distribution of the outcome Y on Y. Formally, for any distribution π(α | x) of A given X = x,

# 4 The AOI estimator 

This section presents the AOI estimator. Section 4.1 introduces the prior and posterior densities that serve as building blocks. Section 4.2 defines the transition matrix Q(x), with entries given by posterior predictive probabilities, and its pseudoinverse. Section 4.3 presents the estimator itself and characterizes the sieve space it implicitly uses. Section 4.4 shows that the estimator arises as the limit of an iterated bias correction. 

## 4.1 Prior, posterior, and predictive densities

# 7 Inference on common parameters 

The main body of this paper focuses on estimation of μ0 in models where the conditional outcome probabilities f (y | x, α ) do not depend on any common parameter. As noted before, many panel models of interest include a finite-dimensional common parameter θ0,so that the outcome probabilities take the form f (y | x, α, θ 0). This section discusses two issues that arise in that setting: how inference on μ0 is affected by the presence of θ0, and how θ0 itself can be estimated. 33 7.1 Inference on μ0 in models with θ0

All results in this paper carry over directly to models with a common parameter θ0,provided a consistent estimator bθ of θ0 is available. Given bθ, one simply evaluates the AOI estimating function at bθ, that is, bμ(∞) = n−1 Pni=1 w(∞)(Yi, X i, bθ), where w(∞)(y, x, θ )is the estimating function from Section 4 applied to the model f (y | x, α, θ ). Consistent estimators of θ0 are available in a wide range of nonlinear panel models. For essentially every type of discrete outcome variable (binary, count data, ordered choice, multinomial choice), there exist model specifications that allow point identification and 

√n-consistent estimation of θ0 even at fixed T . In static models, this is typically achieved through conditional likelihood methods that exploit the existence of a sufficient statis-tic for Ai, as in exponential-family models (Rasch, 1961; Andersen, 1970; Chamberlain, 1980). In dynamic models, appropriate specifications similarly allow estimation of θ0 via generalized method of moments (see, e.g., Honor´ e and Weidner, 2020). More generally, the functional differencing method of Bonhomme (2012) provides a unifying framework for point estimation of θ0 in both static and dynamic panel models. These methods are well established in the literature and widely implemented in statistical software. In models where θ0 is not point-identified at fixed T , the approximate functional differencing (AFD) method of Dhaene and Weidner (2023) can be used to obtain consistent estimators of θ0

whose bias vanishes rapidly as T grows. When θ0 is estimated, the estimation error in bθ may need to be accounted for when conducting inference on μ0. If bθ is √nT -consistent, as is the case for many conditional likelihood and AFD estimators, then √n(bθ − θ0) = OP (T −1/2), and the contribution of the estimation error in bθ to the asymptotic distribution of bμ(∞) vanishes as T → ∞ .In this case, no formal adjustment is required, and the asymptotic theory of Section 5 applies directly. For good finite-sample performance, however, it may still be advisable to account for the estimation error in bθ.More generally, if bθ is √n-consistent and asymptotically linear with influence function

## 7.2 Estimation of θ0

The ideas developed in this paper also apply to inference on θ0 itself. For most of the paper, we have suppressed θ0 from the notation. Reinstating θ0, the AOI construction produces estimating functions w(Yi, X i, θ 0) that satisfy μ0 ≈ E[w(Yi, X i, θ 0)] for large T ,with the bias decaying exponentially under our regularity conditions. Estimation of θ0

calls for moment functions of the same family but targeting zero rather than μ0, that is, functions m(Yi, X i, θ 0) such that 0 ≈ E[m(Yi, X i, θ 0)], which can be combined into a GMM estimator for θ0.The construction of such moment functions follows the same logic as the construction of w in this paper, starting from the score of the MLE for θ and applying the iterated bias correction machinery to the resulting average-effect expression — this construction is detailed, without asymptotic theory, in Dhaene and Weidner (2023). At θ = θ0, the resulting moment functions are themselves average effects in the sense of the present paper, so the bias and variance results of Section 5 apply directly to E[m(Yi, X i, θ 0)] as 

n, T → ∞ . Two ingredients are not addressed in that paper: identification of θ0 from the moment conditions, and well-behavedness of the Jacobian ∇θE[m(Yi, X i, θ )] θ=θ0 . Once these are established, standard GMM cross-sectional asymptotics combined with the bias control developed here yields the asymptotic distribution of the GMM estimator for θ0.Controlling the bias at θ0 is the technically demanding step, and it is precisely what the AOI theory of our paper provides. 

# 8 Conclusion 

This paper develops the approximate operator inversion method (AOI) for estimating average effects in nonlinear panel data models with fixed effects. The central idea is to recast the estimation problem as an inversion of the distributional mapping from the fixed-effect distribution to the outcome distribution. This mapping goes from an infinite-35 dimensional space to a finite-dimensional space, so it cannot be inverted exactly, but the approximation improves as T grows because the outcome space becomes richer. The resulting estimator can be understood as the limit of infinitely iterated large T bias corrections. Two properties of AOI are worth highlighting. First, the bias has a product struc-ture (rate double robustness), decaying at the product of the approximation rates for the average-effect function and the fixed-effect distribution. Under analyticity conditions, this yields exponential bias decay in T , so that only T ≫ log n is needed for valid infer-ence. Second, the method operates entirely at the distributional level and never estimates individual fixed effects. This makes it applicable in what we call regime (C) in the intro-duction, where the fixed effects cannot be consistently estimated, a setting not covered or discussed by any existing papers.
