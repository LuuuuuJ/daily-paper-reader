## Abstract 

Mediation analysis has traditionally focused on outcome-level summary contrasts, such as mean effects, which may obscure substantial distribu-tional changes induced by complex and nonlinear causal mechanisms. We propose Distributional Causal Mediation Analysis (DCMA), a generative learning framework for identifying and estimating treatment effects on entire outcome distributions transmitted through multiple mediators. DCMA learns conditional generative models for the me-diators and the outcome, recovering the relevant conditional distributions from observational data. Leveraging the identification formulas, it recon-structs interventional outcome distributions via Monte Carlo forward simulation by noise resam-pling, enabling the capture of both classical sum-mary effects and rich distributional contrasts such as energy distance and the Wasserstein distance. Analytical error bounds are derived to decompose how estimation errors in the learned conditional models propagate to the reconstructed interven-tional outcome distributions. The empirical ef-fectiveness of DCMA is demonstrated through numerical experiments and real-world data appli-cations.

Interventions with similar aver-age effects can yield very different outcome distributions, for example by affecting subgroups differently. A track-ing program, through differences in teacher support and resources, may leave the class average unchanged while widening the gap between high and low performers, result-ing in a bimodal distribution. Therefore, it is essential to move beyond summary-based mediation analysis toward a full distributional characterization of interventional effects. Moreover, in practice there are typically multiple mediators, raising the question of how to capture the path-specific dis-tributional effects of an exposure on an outcome that operate through distinct mediators. Motivated by above considerations, we extend interven-tional mediation analysis to a distributional framework that characterizes how interventions reshape the entire outcome distribution, both directly and through multiple mediators. 1

We further introduce a generalized notion of distributional mediation effects that unifies a broad class of causal targets, including mean and quantile effects, exceedance probabili-ties, and distributional distances such as the energy distance (ED) (Rizzo & Sz´ ekely, 2016). We derive identification results that express distributional in-terventional effects in terms of conditional distributions, re-ducing estimation to learning the conditional laws of media-tors and outcomes under appropriate interventions. Building on this characterization, we develop a deep generative learn-ing framework that flexibly models these conditional distri-butions via a conditional generator g(X, ε ), enabling effi-cient Monte Carlo approximation of interventional outcome distributions without explicit likelihood specification (Zhou et al., 2023; Song et al., 2025; Shen & Meinshausen, 2025). Leveraging these insights, we propose the Distributional Causal Mediation Analysis (DCMA) framework. Our con-tributions are threefold: • We introduce distributional interventional mediation estimands that characterize causal mediation effects at the level of full outcome distributions, generalizing classical mean-based mediation effects. • We develop a generative mediation framework that models the conditional distributions of multiple media-tors and outcome via noise-based conditional genera-tors, enabling flexible distributional effect estimation. • We establish identification results and derive analytical error bounds that quantify how estimation errors in the learned mediator and outcome conditional models propagate to the reconstructed interventional outcome distributions. 

## 2. Related Works on Interventional Mediation 

Interventional mediation was formally introduced to en-able effect decomposition in settings with exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounding, where natural mediation effects may be ill-defined or non-identifiable (VanderWeele et al., 2014). By defining mediation effects through interven-tions on mediator distributions rather than fixing mediators at counterfactual values, this framework avoids reliance on cross-world counterfactual assumptions. Building on this formulation, subsequent work extended in-terventional mediation to increasingly complex settings, in-cluding multiple mediators with unknown causal structures (Vansteelandt & Daniel, 2017; Lin & VanderWeele, 2017; Loh et al., 2022a), longitudinal settings with time-varying exposures and mediators (VanderWeele & Tchetgen Tch-etgen, 2017), and nonlinear models with high-dimensional mediators (Loh et al., 2022b). More recently, (Moreno-Betancur et al., 2021) further enriched the conceptual frame-work of interventional mediation by aligning it with target trial emulation, providing a policy-oriented interpretation through hypothetical shifts in mediator distributions. Along related lines, Benkeser & Ran (2021) proposed non-parametric and semiparametric estimation and inference for interventional mediation effects. Building on earlier simulation-based strategies for mediation analysis (Imai et al., 2010), Zhou & Wodtke (2025) develop a Monte Carlo–based framework for interventional mediation effects using normalizing flows as flexible conditional density mod-els. However, their analysis still focuses on mean-based effects, does not account for differences in entire outcome distributions, and lacks theoretical convergence guarantees.

5.1. ED and its RKHS representation 

We use the ED (Rizzo & Sz ´ekely, 2016) to quantify distri-butional estimation error as it is closely aligned with the ES used for training. Specifically, the expected ES of a predic-tive distribution differs from its ED to the true distribution only by a constant that depends on the true distribution. For any P, Q ∈ P 1(Rd), the ED is defined as 

ED( P, Q ) = 2 E∥X − Y ∥ − E∥X − X′∥ − E∥Y − Y ′∥,

## 7. Discussion and Conclusion 

In summary, DCMA provides a principled generative frame-work for distributional causal mediation analysis, enabling the estimation of mediation effects beyond conventional mean contrasts. By characterizing interventional effects at the level of entire outcome distributions, DCMA offers ad-ditional insights into complex and heterogeneous mediation mechanisms that are not captured by mean-based analyses. DCMA reconstructs interventional outcome distributions via Monte Carlo forward simulation by resampling exoge-nous noise and plugging it into the trained conditional gen-erators, a mechanism that naturally aligns with structural causal models and do-interventions. By training with proper distribution discrepancies, DCMA ensures consistent esti-mation of the underlying interventional outcome distribu-tions. While alternative generative models such as normaliz-ing flows or diffusion models could also be incorporated, our main contribution lies in establishing a causally aligned, the-oretically grounded, and computationally stable framework for distributional causal mediation analysis.

## E. Details of the Real Data Study 

We applied the proposed framework to data from the UK Biobank (UKB), a large population-based cohort of over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years, recruited between 2006 and 2010. Individuals with complete data on smoking status, serum total cholesterol, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), age, sex, and the Townsend deprivation index were included. After applying these inclusion criteria, the final analytical sample consisted of 18,318 participants. ## F. Ablation Study 

To assess whether distributional recovery truly requires a flexible outcome generator, we conduct a targeted ablation within the DCMA framework under the bimodal outcome scenario S1. We compare the full DCMA model with a variant in which the outcome generator is replaced by a linear Gaussian model, while keeping the mediator generator unchanged. A linear Gaussian outcome model is inherently centered on conditional mean recovery under a unimodal normality assumption. Even when used for Monte Carlo sampling, it can only produce Gaussian outcomes and is therefore incapable of representing bimodal or more complex interventional outcome distributions. This mismatch is clearly illustrated in Figure 7, where the ablated model generates only unimodal Gaussian samples that fail to match the true bimodal structure. The consequence of this limitation is quantified in Figure 8.
