# Adaptive Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation of the Mean Potential Outcome under a Treatment Rule 

## Yichen Xu 1 and Mark J. van der Laan 11Division of Biostatistics, University of California, Berkeley, yichen xu@berkeley.edu, laan@berkeley.edu 

Abstract 

Estimating the mean counterfactual outcome under a treatment rule is a central problem in causal inference and policy evaluation. Standard estimators, including inverse probability weighting (IPW), augmented IPW (AIPW), and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE), can become unstable under practical positivity violations because their targeting or weighting steps depend on inverse propen-sity scores. We propose an adaptive targeted maximum likelihood estimation (A-TMLE) framework that uses a data-adaptive working model for the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). This working model induces a projected policy-value parameter, which coincides with the nonparametric mean poten-tial outcome when the CATE is well represented by the adaptive basis. We derive the efficient influence function for the projected parameter and characterize its second-order remainder. We also introduce a regularized TMLE that targets the nonparametric policy value using a stabilized targeting covariate obtained by projecting the standard TMLE clever covariate onto the score space induced by the CATE working model. We quantify the first-order plug-in bias of regularized TMLE relative to the nonpara-metric target. The resulting targeting steps avoid direct inverse propensity score weighting, improving stability under limited overlap. In simulations, A-TMLE and regularized TMLE achieve lower mean squared error and improved coverage compared with IPW, AIPW, and standard TMLE under practical positivity violations, while remaining competitive when treatment overlap is strong. A real-data appli-cation to the Right Heart Catheterization study illustrates that the adaptive estimators produce stable policy-value estimates with substantially shorter confidence intervals than IPW and AIPW. 

# 1 Introduction 

A central goal in personalized medicine and causal decision making is to identify treatment strategies that optimize patient outcomes. Such strategies are commonly formalized as dynamic treatment regimes or in-dividualized treatment rules, which map patient characteristics or evolving treatment histories to treatment decisions Murphy [2003]. The performance of a treatment rule is typically summarized by the mean coun-terfactual outcome under the rule, representing the expected outcome that would be observed if the entire population were treated according to that strategy. Estimation and inference for this value parameter have been widely studied in the causal inference and biostatistics literature. Early work focused on learning op-timal treatment regimes from longitudinal data using potential outcome frameworks and sequential decision methods Murphy [2003]. More recent developments have emphasized semiparametric approaches that avoid 1

> arXiv:2605.01671v1 [stat.ME] 3 May 2026

restrictive parametric assumptions, including targeted learning methods for estimating the mean outcome under optimal dynamic treatment rules and for conducting statistical inference in large nonparametric models van der Laan and Luedtke [2015]. Extensions of this framework have addressed settings with additional con-straints, such as limited treatment resources, and have developed efficient estimators for the value of optimal individualized treatment rules Luedtke and van der Laan [2016]. In longitudinal studies with time-varying treatments, estimation of counterfactual mean outcomes often relies on representations of the longitudinal g-formula together with doubly robust or sequentially doubly robust estimators that remain consistent when either outcome models or treatment mechanisms are correctly specified Luedtke et al. [2018], Tran et al. [2019]. Parallel developments in statistics and machine learning have focused on directly learning optimal individualized treatment rules by framing the problem as a weighted classification task, leading to methods such as outcome weighted learning and its extensions based on residual weighting Zhao et al. [2012], Zhou et al. [2017]. Together, these approaches provide complementary tools for estimating and evaluating treat-ment strategies and for understanding the population mean outcomes that would result under alternative intervention rules. In many applications, treatment strategies must be evaluated using observational data, where treatment assignment is determined by clinical practice, patient characteristics, or system-level factors rather than ran-domized allocation. While randomized controlled trials provide strong internal validity, observational studies often leverage larger and more heterogeneous populations and reflect real-world clinical settings. However, the absence of randomization requires additional assumptions to identify causal quantities such as the mean counterfactual outcome under a treatment rule. In particular, identification and estimation generally rely on models for either the outcome regression, as in g-computation Robins [1986], or the treatment mechanism through propensity score–based approaches Hern´ an and Robins [2016]. Many commonly used estimators, including inverse probability weighting and doubly robust procedures such as targeted maximum likelihood estimation van der Laan and Rose [2011], depend critically on the positivity assumption. Formally, if g(a | W ) = P (A = a | W ) denotes the treatment mechanism, positivity requires 0 < g (a |

W ) < 1 for all treatment levels a and covariate values W with positive probability. This condition ensures that all treatment options under consideration are sufficiently represented in the observed data so that the mean counterfactual outcome under a treatment rule can be identified and estimated. Violations of positivity can arise either structurally, when certain treatments are impossible for a subgroup, or practically, when treatment assignment is theoretically possible but rarely observed in finite samples. Practical violations are common in high-dimensional or highly stratified settings and may lead to unstable estimators, for example through extreme inverse propensity weights 1 /g (A | W ) that inflate variance and introduce finite-sample bias Crump et al. [2006, 2009]. Various strategies have been proposed to mitigate these issues, including truncation of estimated propensity scores, restricting analyses to regions of sufficient overlap, or using alternative weighting schemes that emphasize covariate regions with better treatment overlap Cole and Hern´ an [2008], Zhou et al. [2020]. Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE) van der Laan and Rubin [2006], van der Laan and Rose [2011, 2018], Gruber and Laan [2012] is a general semiparametric framework for estimating causal parameters by combining flexible machine learning estimation of nuisance functions with a targeted updating step based on the efficient influence function, yielding estimators that are doubly robust and asymptotically efficient Smith et al. [2023]. A substantial body of work has focused on improving the stability of TMLE when estimating average treatment effects in the presence of practical positivity violations, where small estimated treatment probabilities lead to unstable inverse probability weights and highly variable clever covariates. For 2example, truncation of inverse probability weights has been proposed to control variance and mean squared error Gruber et al. [2022], Xu et al. [2026], while recent adaptive targeted learning approaches project the target parameter onto data-adaptive working models using relaxed highly adaptive lasso structures Benkeser and Van Der Laan [2016], van der Laan et al. [2023, 2025]. Other contributions address inference and variance estimation, such as plug-in and targeted bootstrap procedures designed to correct underestimation of efficient influence function–based variance estimators Tran et al. [2023]. Beyond the average treatment effect, the targeted learning framework also provides estimators for sequential decision problems and dynamic treatment regimes through longitudinal TMLE (L-TMLE), which estimates the mean counterfactual outcome under static or dynamic treatment rules van der Laan and Rose [2011, 2018]. Despite these methodological advances, the impact of practical positivity violations on TMLE-type estimators for policy evaluation—that is, estimation of the mean counterfactual outcome under a treatment rule—has received comparatively less systematic investigation. In this article, we study estimation of the mean potential outcome under a fixed treatment rule d in a point-treatment setting, with particular focus on practical positivity violations. Rather than targeting the fully nonparametric parameter E[Y d] directly, we develop a semiparametric projection framework induced by a working model for the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). Specifically, we use the highly adaptive lasso (HAL) to construct a data-adaptive basis for the CATE and define a projected policy-value parameter that coincides with E[Y d] when the CATE is contained in the working model. Based on this formulation, we propose A-TMLE, which estimates the projected parameter without using inverse propen-sity score weights, and regularized TMLE, which targets the nonparametric policy value using a stabilized outcome-targeting step based on a projected clever covariate. We derive the efficient influence function (EIF) for the projected parameter and characterize its second-order remainder. We also show that the regularized TMLE clever covariate is the L2-projection of the standard TMLE clever covariate onto the tangent space induced by the CATE working model, and quantify the first-order plug-in bias of regularized TMLE relative to the nonparametric target. For inference, we construct Wald confidence intervals using influence-function-based standard errors. Through simulations across sample sizes and treatment policies, we show that the proposed A-TMLE and regularized TMLE estimators achieve lower mean squared error and improved cover-age compared with standard TMLE, augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) Robins et al. [1994], and inverse probability weighting (IPW) Horvitz and Thompson [1952] under practical positivity violations, while remaining competitive when treatment overlap is strong.

# 5 Real data application 

We applied the proposed methods to the Right Heart Catheterization (RHC) study, an observational study of critically ill patients originally analyzed by Connors et al. [1996]. Right heart catheterization is a di-agnostic procedure used to directly measure cardiac function in critically ill patients. In this dataset, the treatment indicator records whether RHC was applied within 24 hours of admission, and the outcome records 30-day survival. We used survival as the outcome, defined as Y = 1 − death , and used swang1 as the bi-nary treatment. After removing observations with missing treatment or outcome values, we constructed the covariate vector W from baseline variables after excluding treatment, outcome, follow-up survival, and prognosis variables. The treatment mechanism ˆ g(W ) and outcome regression ˆQ(A, W ) were estimated us-ing Super Learner, and the CATE working model was estimated using the relaxed HAL-based procedure described above. We evaluated deterministic treatment rules based on the APACHE score aps1 , a measure of baseline illness severity. For cutoffs c ranging over the middle 80% of the empirical aps1 distribution, we considered policies of the form 

dc(W ) = 1{APS1 > c }.

Thus, patients with APACHE score above the cutoff are assigned to receive RHC, while those below the cutoff are assigned not to receive RHC. For each cutoff, we estimated the mean survival under the rule using IPW, AIPW, A-TMLE, and regularized TMLE. The A-TMLE and regularized TMLE estimates are close, with regularized TMLE generally shifting A-TMLE slightly toward AIPW. # 6 Discussion 

In this work, we developed an adaptive targeted maximum likelihood estimation (A-TMLE) framework for policy-value estimation under practical positivity violations. The proposed approach replaces unstable inverse-propensity-weighted targeting directions with projected directions induced by a data-adaptive CATE working model. The resulting estimators are designed to improve stability under limited overlap, while reg-ularized TMLE further uses an outcome-targeting step to reduce first-order plug-in bias when the projected parameter is used as an approximation to the full nonparametric target E[Y d]. Across simulations and the RHC data application, the adaptive estimators showed improved finite-sample stability relative to IPW, AIPW, and standard TMLE in settings with practical positivity concerns. Several limitations remain. First, the primary A-TMLE estimand is a projected policy-value parameter rather than the unrestricted nonparametric policy value, except when the CATE is well represented by the working model. Thus, interpretation depends on the quality of the CATE projection. Although regularized TMLE can reduce plug-in bias toward the nonparametric target, its theoretical justification requires the projected EIF to approximate the nonparametric EIF sufficiently well. Second, the finite-sample performance depends on the choice of adaptive basis, the relaxed HAL fit, and regularization. More systematic procedures for selecting the projection dimension or controlling the approximation–variance tradeoff would strengthen practical implementation. Third, inference remains an important issue: our simulations suggest that standard TMLE influence-function-based intervals can improve coverage, but a more complete theory for variance estimation under data-adaptive projection is needed. Future work should extend this framework to longitudinal treatment settings with time-varying covariates and dynamic treatment regimes. In such problems, practical positivity violations can be substantially more severe because treatment probabilities accumulate over time, making stable projected targeting especially useful. Another direction is to develop data-adaptive criteria for choosing the CATE working model and for deciding when the projected target is close enough to the nonparametric policy value. Finally, bootstrap or higher-order inference procedures may further improve uncertainty quantification for adaptive projection-based TMLE estimators. 17 References
